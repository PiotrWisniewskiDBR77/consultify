# What Executives Should Require Before Approving the Next Wave of Change

Target persona: CEO / executive sponsor / board-facing transformation lead  
Funnel stage: Decision  
Core problem: the next wave of change often gets approved on momentum and narrative confidence, which loads capacity before value proof, ownership, and dependency risk are honest  
Main promise: executives can gate the next wave with a short evidence standard that covers value proof, capacity, governance load, risk response readiness, and portfolio trade-offs already recorded

The next wave of change is where transformations quietly become irreversible. Announcements create expectations. Headlines imply commitment. Teams rearrange priorities before the operating system is ready.

That makes the approval moment one of the highest-leverage control points executives still own—if they treat it as a gate, not a ceremony. A gate is short, explicit, and uncomfortable on purpose. It forces the organization to show what will be displaced, what is already true, and what will happen within weeks if control signals degrade.

> **What executives should refuse to approve without:** trailing proof tied to initiatives, honest capacity and ownership for cross-functional work, governance that fits decision SLAs, risk readiness with triggers—and a stop-doing list that funds the new wave.

## The failure mode: momentum approval

Momentum approval happens for understandable reasons. The last deck looked confident. Competitors are “doing AI” or “doing transformation.” Pausing would embarrass a sponsor. The program office promises to “figure out capacity later.”

That pattern raises governance debt and stretches execution discipline until the portfolio cracks—usually in the form of late surprises, overloaded names on green plans, and escalating narrative polish.

## Executive approval checklist: non-negotiable minimum

Use this as a gate. Treat missing items as a pause, not a secret workaround.

1. **Value proof packet:** trailing metrics aligned to the value case, with variance explained in plain language.  
2. **Dependency map:** top cross-functional dependencies with owners and aging status.  
3. **Capacity truth:** where hours and leadership airtime come from, by function—not only FTE counts.  
4. **Governance fit:** forum map passes the decision test; no net forum inflation without retirements.  
5. **Risk readiness:** top risks have triggers, owners, and dated responses.  
6. **Portfolio trade-off record:** what stops, pauses, or merges to fund the wave.  
7. **Intervention plan:** what happens within two weeks if decision latency breaches thresholds.

If the team cannot produce these artifacts, the organization is not ready. It may still be eager. Eagerness is not evidence.

## Momentum approval versus evidence approval

| Approval style | Basis | Typical portfolio outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Momentum | Narrative confidence | Overload and late surprises |
| Evidence | Proof, capacity, governance | Uncomfortable gates, better control |

Evidence approval is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It is how executives protect teams from being set up to fail in public.

## What executives should refuse

Refuse a wave plan without a stop-doing list tied to capacity. Refuse “green” status without delivery truth and dependency aging. Refuse new initiatives added without a defunding or merge decision elsewhere. Refuse expanded scope without updated ROI assumptions and kill signals.

These refusals are easier when they are standard. Predictable gates reduce politics because everyone knows the rules before the room fills.

## Why appetite is not capacity

Wave approval often breaks when leaders treat portfolio appetite as evidence of capacity. The strategy sounds coherent. The sponsor sounds committed. The next wave sounds smaller than the first.

But if no one can show what work, airtime, and decision bandwidth will actually be displaced, the organization is not approving a wave. It is approving overlap—and overlap is how execution discipline dies quietly.
