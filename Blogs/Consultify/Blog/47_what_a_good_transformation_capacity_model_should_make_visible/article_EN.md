# What a Good Transformation Capacity Model Should Make Visible

Target persona: Transformation COO / portfolio lead / HR business partner supporting change  
Funnel stage: Awareness  
Core problem: plans stay green while named people carry impossible loads, so execution slips and sponsors misread the problem as motivation instead of capacity math  
Main promise: you can define a capacity model that exposes role load, contention, and governance drag in the same view as delivery milestones

Capacity is the hidden constraint in most transformations. Headcount charts lie politely. Budget totals smooth away contention. FTE math imagines interchangeable hours.

Execution, however, runs through named people, specific skills, finite leadership airtime, and dependency queues that burn capacity even when tasks look “in progress.” A good capacity model makes that reality visible in the same place sponsors already look for portfolio health—so trade-offs become unavoidable before heroics become culture.

> **What leadership should see in one view:** named load, critical-path contention, governance drag, saturation by team, dependency wait time, and reserve for intervention work.

## Six visibility layers that change decisions

**1. Named assignment load**  
Who is accountable on how many initiatives, with what weekly hours booked—not “we have five people.”

**2. Critical-path contention**  
Where one person blocks multiple streams simultaneously, and what relief options exist (pause, merge, borrow, reprioritize).

**3. Governance drag**  
Hours spent in steering, prep, and status production versus recorded decision output. Drag is a capacity line item.

**4. Change saturation**  
How many concurrent changes hit the same operational teams in the same month. Saturation is a sequencing input, not a communications problem alone.

**5. Dependency queue time**  
Where work waits on decisions or external releases even though teams are busy. Waiting is not idle; it consumes attention and schedule.

**6. Intervention reserve**  
Buffer capacity reserved for stabilization, rework, and risk response. If every hour is booked to “plan,” the plan has no shock absorber.

## The green plan test: three credibility checks

A plan is not credible until it passes these checks:

| Check | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Named reality | Does every critical role have a named person with hours—not a role family? |
| Contention | Does any name appear on more than two critical paths without explicit relief? |
| Governance | Does sponsor time include prep and follow-ups, not only meeting length? |

If the plan fails these checks, “green” is a reporting artifact until capacity is repaired.

## Checklist: the model is decision-grade when

- Overloaded names trigger portfolio trade-offs, not pep talks.  
- PMO load is visible alongside delivery team load.  
- Saturation informs sequencing, not only communications volume.  
- Capacity assumptions appear in the assumption ledger.  
- Staffing changes produce updated plans within one review cycle.

## Why averaging hides the break point

Capacity models fail when they stop naming people and start averaging pain. The spreadsheet can look balanced. Headcount can look sufficient. Monthly capacity can look rational.

Once contention disappears into averages, leadership cannot see which names carry the hidden conflict that will break execution first. Naming is not micromanagement. It is how portfolios discover where “no” must happen.
